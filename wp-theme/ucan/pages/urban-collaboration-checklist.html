Practical framework

Urban Collaboration Checklist: A Practical Framework for Cities

A practical framework for deciding whether, and how, government, community organisations, researchers and funders should work together on an urban problem.

By U-CAN editorial team · Published 19 September 2026 · Updated 19 September 2026 · 16 min read

What is the Urban Collaboration Checklist?

The Urban Collaboration Checklist is a practical framework for assessing whether organisations or institutions should collaborate on an urban problem and, if so, how to structure the partnership. It runs from defining the problem and testing readiness to agreeing roles, checking resources and measuring outcomes rather than meetings.

Key takeaways

  • Ask first whether collaboration is needed: some problems are better solved by one capable organisation.
  • The checklist has 10 stages, from defining the problem to measuring outcomes rather than meetings.
  • Test readiness and agree roles, decisions and resources before announcing a partnership.
  • A one-page version and a 10-minute test make the framework usable in a real meeting.

Urban problems rarely arrive one at a time.

A city trying to improve public transport may also be dealing with land-use planning, pedestrian safety, informal livelihoods, air pollution and access for women and older people.

A neighbourhood improvement project may involve municipal departments, residents, community organisations, planners, elected representatives, researchers and private partners.

A climate-resilience programme may require cooperation across water, sanitation, housing, transport, disaster management and environmental agencies.

Yet urban institutions and organisations often work within separate mandates, budgets and programmes.

That creates a practical question:

How can different organisations and institutions collaborate effectively around a shared urban problem?

The answer is not simply to organise another meeting.

Collaboration needs structure.

This is why cities and urban organisations can benefit from an Urban Collaboration Checklist—a practical framework for deciding when to collaborate, whom to involve, how to structure the partnership, and how to determine whether the collaboration is actually producing value.

What is urban collaboration?

Urban collaboration is the process through which different actors work together to address a shared city challenge.

Those actors may include:

  • Municipal governments
  • State government departments
  • Elected representatives
  • NGOs
  • Community organisations
  • Researchers
  • Universities
  • Urban practitioners
  • Philanthropic organisations
  • Businesses and social enterprises
  • Professional associations
  • Citizens and resident groups

Collaboration does not necessarily mean that organisations give up their independence.

It means that they deliberately combine some combination of:

knowledge + relationships + resources + capabilities + authority + implementation capacity

around a defined objective.

This distinction matters.

A city can have many organisations working on the same issue without actually having collaboration.

Why urban collaboration is difficult

The case for collaboration is easy to make.

The practice is harder.

U-CAN's Request for Collaboration initiative identifies several reasons: organisations often specialise in different parts of larger urban challenges, while funding structures and organisational systems can favour individual, project-based delivery. Collaboration also requires time, trust and flexibility.

This produces a familiar pattern.

Two organisations may:

  1. agree that a problem matters;
  2. like each other's work;
  3. believe collaboration would be useful;
  4. hold several meetings;
  5. discuss possible projects;

…and still never build something together.

The gap between interest and joint action is where many collaborations fail.

An urban collaboration checklist is designed to make that gap visible before significant resources are committed.

The Urban Collaboration Checklist

A useful collaboration can be examined through 10 stages:

  1. Define the problem
  2. Establish the shared purpose
  3. Identify the right partners
  4. Test collaboration readiness
  5. Define complementary strengths
  6. Agree on roles and decision-making
  7. Secure resources and capacity
  8. Design the collaboration
  9. Learn and adapt
  10. Measure outcomes and decide what happens next

These stages should not necessarily be treated as a rigid linear process.

Urban problems change.

Partnerships evolve.

New information can change the original diagnosis.

The checklist is therefore better understood as a decision and learning framework.

1. Define the problem before defining the partnership

One of the most common mistakes is beginning with:

"Who can we partner with?"

The better starting point is:

"What problem are we actually trying to solve?"

Before identifying partners, answer:

Problem checklist

  • Is the problem clearly defined?
  • Is there evidence that the problem exists?
  • Who experiences the problem?
  • Where does it occur?
  • What are its immediate causes?
  • What are its systemic causes?
  • Which parts of the problem are already being addressed?
  • What remains unresolved?
  • Is the problem genuinely too large, complex or interconnected for one actor to address effectively?

That final question is particularly important.

Not every project needs collaboration.

Sometimes one organisation can solve a problem efficiently on its own.

Collaboration makes greater sense when different actors possess capabilities that are genuinely complementary.

2. Establish a shared purpose

Partners can agree on a problem without agreeing on what success means.

For example:

"Improve urban mobility" is a broad ambition.

A collaboration might instead agree to:

Improve safe and affordable access to public transport for workers in three selected neighbourhoods.

A good shared purpose should clarify:

  • What will change?
  • For whom?
  • Where?
  • By when?
  • What evidence would demonstrate progress?

Shared-purpose checklist

  • Do all partners describe the problem in broadly compatible terms?
  • Is there agreement about the intended outcome?
  • Are expectations realistic?
  • Is the geography defined?
  • Is the target population defined where relevant?
  • Is the time horizon clear?
  • Are there disagreements that need to be resolved before proceeding?

If partners cannot agree on the destination, defining the route together will be difficult.

3. Identify the right partners—not just familiar ones

Collaboration should not become a networking exercise in which organisations partner simply because they already know each other.

Start by mapping capabilities.

For example:

CapabilityPotential partner
Community engagementCommunity organisation
Urban planningPlanning institution
DataResearch/technology organisation
Municipal accessGovernment/urban institution
Policy analysisThink tank
ImplementationNGO/social enterprise
FinancingFunder/philanthropic organisation
CommunicationsCommunications partner

The objective is complementarity, not maximum membership.

Ask:

What capability does each partner bring that the others do not already possess?

If the answer is "nothing significant," collaboration may not be necessary.

4. Test whether the organisations are ready to collaborate

Good intentions are not enough.

Before committing, organisations should examine their readiness.

U-CAN's recent collaboration work identifies five practical signals observed through its Request for Collaboration experience: shared curiosity about the problem, recognition of each other's strengths, leadership-level trust, a genuine trigger to move from discussion to action, and internal capacity to sustain the work.

These provide a useful starting point for a readiness conversation.

Collaboration-readiness checklist

  • Are we genuinely curious about the problem?
  • Do we understand and respect the other organisation's strengths?
  • Is there sufficient trust between leadership teams?
  • Is there a real reason to act now?
  • Do both organisations have the internal capacity to participate?
  • Can we disagree without damaging the relationship?
  • Are we willing to modify our original assumptions?
  • Are we prepared for collaboration to take longer than a normal project?

A partnership should not necessarily proceed just because every box is checked.

The checklist is intended to surface conversations that might otherwise happen too late.

5. Identify what each partner contributes

Once partners decide to explore collaboration, make contributions explicit.

For each organisation, document:

What do we bring?

This could be:

  • technical expertise;
  • local relationships;
  • data;
  • research;
  • funding;
  • staff;
  • technology;
  • community trust;
  • government relationships;
  • implementation experience;
  • communications;
  • convening capacity.

Then ask:

What do we need from others?

This exposes the actual logic of the partnership.

A collaboration becomes stronger when each partner can explain why the others are necessary.

6. Agree on roles and decision-making

Many collaborations begin with enthusiasm and fail later because nobody knows who decides what.

Before implementation, establish:

Decision checklist

  • Who owns the overall initiative?
  • Who manages day-to-day coordination?
  • Who approves major changes?
  • Who controls shared funds?
  • Who owns or manages shared data?
  • How are disagreements resolved?
  • How frequently will partners meet?
  • Who communicates externally?
  • Who is accountable for each major deliverable?

A simple responsibility matrix can help.

ActivityLeadSupporting partnerDecision-maker
Problem definitionOrganisation AB, CJoint
Field researchBA, CB
Pilot designA + BCJoint
Government engagementCA, BAgreed lead
EvaluationBA, CJoint

The exact structure will vary.

The important principle is clarity.

7. Check resources before announcing the partnership

Collaboration requires resources even when no major new programme is being created.

Resources may include:

  • Staff time
  • Travel
  • Research
  • Data
  • Meetings
  • Technology
  • Facilitation
  • Communications
  • Legal support
  • Monitoring and evaluation
  • Administrative coordination

A common mistake is assuming that collaboration is "free" because organisations are contributing existing staff.

Staff time is a resource.

Resource checklist

  • Is there a named person responsible for the collaboration?
  • Is sufficient staff time available?
  • Is there a budget?
  • Are funding responsibilities clear?
  • Are administrative responsibilities assigned?
  • Is there flexibility to change the plan?
  • What happens if additional resources are required?

8. Design the collaboration as a small experiment where possible

Not every collaboration needs to begin with a large programme.

For uncertain partnerships, a limited pilot can answer important questions.

For example:

Phase 1: Joint problem diagnosis

Phase 2: Small pilot

Phase 3: Reflection

Phase 4: Adaptation

Phase 5: Decision on expansion

This approach can reduce the risk of committing significant resources before partners understand how they actually work together.

U-CAN's Request for Collaboration was explicitly designed around experimentation, reflection and shared learning rather than prescribing a single solution in advance.

That is particularly relevant for complex urban problems where the right solution may not be obvious at the beginning.

9. Build learning into the collaboration

A partnership should not wait until the end to ask:

"Did it work?"

Learning should happen throughout the process.

After important milestones, partners can ask:

Learning checklist

  • What did we expect?
  • What actually happened?
  • What surprised us?
  • What assumptions proved incorrect?
  • What did each partner learn?
  • What did communities tell us?
  • What did government stakeholders tell us?
  • What should we change?
  • What should we stop?
  • What should we continue?

This transforms collaboration from a delivery mechanism into a learning mechanism.

10. Measure outcomes—not meetings

A collaboration can have:

  • regular meetings;
  • a WhatsApp group;
  • workshops;
  • reports;
  • conferences;
  • newsletters;

and still have little effect on the underlying problem.

Therefore, measurement should distinguish between activity, collaboration and outcome.

Level 1: Activity

  • Number of meetings
  • Participants
  • Workshops
  • Research outputs

Level 2: Collaboration

  • Joint initiatives
  • Shared resources
  • Joint problem-solving
  • Cross-organisational learning
  • New institutional relationships

Level 3: Outcome

  • Improved service
  • Better policy implementation
  • Increased access
  • Reduced problem severity
  • Improved institutional capability
  • Measurable community benefit

The higher levels are generally more informative about whether collaboration is producing value.

The 10-minute Urban Collaboration Test

Before signing an MoU or launching a joint project, partners should be able to answer these questions quickly.

The problem

1. What problem are we solving?

The people

2. Who is affected?

The purpose

3. What change are we trying to create?

The reason

4. Why can't each organisation reasonably do this alone?

The partners

5. Why are these particular organisations involved?

The contribution

6. What does each partner uniquely contribute?

The trigger

7. Why collaborate now?

The trust

8. What assumptions or concerns could undermine the partnership?

The capacity

9. Who has the time and resources to do the work?

The outcome

10. What will be different if the collaboration succeeds?

If these questions produce vague answers, the partnership may need more preparation before implementation.

An Urban Collaboration Scorecard—without turning collaboration into a score

A checklist should not become another artificial scoring system.

Instead of giving a partnership a numerical "collaboration score," organisations can classify each area as:

Ready

Needs discussion

Not yet ready

For example:

AreaStatusQuestion
Shared problemReadyDo we understand the problem similarly?
Shared purposeNeeds discussionAre expected outcomes aligned?
Complementary capabilitiesReadyDoes each partner add something distinctive?
TrustNeeds discussionCan we disagree constructively?
Leadership supportReadyAre decision-makers committed?
Staff capacityNot yet readyDo people have time to participate?
ResourcesNeeds discussionWho funds what?
Decision-makingNot yet readyWho decides when partners disagree?
LearningReadyHave we created reflection points?
Outcome measurementNeeds discussionHow will we know whether the work matters?

The value is not the label.

The value is the conversation that produces it.

The government question

Urban collaboration becomes particularly important when a project interacts with government.

Before approaching a municipality or government department, partners should clarify:

  • Which institution has the relevant mandate?
  • Which department needs to be involved?
  • Who has decision-making authority?
  • Who will implement the proposed change?
  • What evidence does government need?
  • Are elected representatives relevant?
  • Are community representatives involved?
  • What existing programme or policy does the collaboration connect to?
  • What happens after the external partners leave?

That last question is crucial.

A successful pilot that cannot be absorbed into existing institutions may remain a pilot indefinitely.

The community question

Collaboration between organisations is not automatically collaboration with communities.

If a project affects residents, workers or vulnerable groups, their knowledge should be considered part of the problem-solving process where appropriate.

Ask:

  • Who experiences the problem?
  • Who has not been represented?
  • What local knowledge already exists?
  • How will feedback influence decisions?
  • How will participation be made meaningful rather than merely procedural?
  • What happens when community priorities differ from institutional priorities?

This prevents "multi-stakeholder collaboration" from becoming a conversation exclusively among professionals.

The data question

Partners increasingly share data, dashboards, surveys and research.

That creates another set of questions.

  • What data is necessary?
  • Who owns it?
  • Who can access it?
  • Can it be shared legally and ethically?
  • How will sensitive information be protected?
  • Are data definitions consistent?
  • How will disagreements over evidence be resolved?

A collaboration should not create unnecessary data collection simply because partners have access to technology.

The principle should be:

Collect what is necessary, use it responsibly and make its limitations clear.

When collaboration may not be the answer

An honest framework should also identify situations where collaboration may not be appropriate.

Collaboration may add unnecessary complexity when:

  • one organisation already has the required capability;
  • the problem is clearly defined and operationally simple;
  • partners have fundamentally incompatible objectives;
  • no organisation has the capacity to participate;
  • the partnership exists mainly for reputational reasons;
  • the transaction costs exceed the likely value;
  • responsibilities cannot be clearly assigned.

More collaboration is not automatically better.

The objective is useful collaboration, not collaboration for its own sake.

What cities can learn from collaboration networks

A city does not necessarily need one giant collaboration platform.

It may need several overlapping forms of collaboration:

Government-to-government

Different departments coordinate around interconnected problems.

Government-to-community

Institutions work with residents and community organisations.

NGO-to-NGO

Organisations combine complementary capabilities.

Research-to-practice

Researchers and practitioners exchange evidence and implementation knowledge.

City-to-city

Practitioners learn from experiences in other cities.

Public-private

Government and businesses collaborate around defined public objectives.

The most effective urban ecosystems may therefore resemble networks of networks, rather than one central partnership.

U-CAN and the practice of collaboration

The Urban Collective Action Network provides a useful Indian example of an ecosystem explicitly focused on collaboration.

U-CAN says its purpose is to create conditions for collaboration between organisations because India's urban challenges are too complex for a single organisation to solve alone. Its network brings together urban practitioners, government officials, researchers and philanthropies.

Its Request for Collaboration is particularly relevant to this framework because it focuses on moving collaboration from intent to practice through experimentation, reflection and shared learning.

U-CAN also reports using mechanisms such as Communities of Learning, City Mixers, fellowships and collaborative initiatives to connect practitioners and organisations across cities.

These experiences suggest an important principle:

Collaboration is not an event. It is a capability that organisations and city ecosystems can develop.

The Urban Collaboration Checklist: One-page version

For practical use, the entire framework can be reduced to this checklist.

BEFORE COLLABORATING

  • Is the problem clearly defined?
  • Is there evidence supporting the diagnosis?
  • Is collaboration actually necessary?
  • Is there a shared purpose?
  • Are the right partners involved?

BEFORE COMMITTING

  • Do partners trust one another sufficiently?
  • Do they understand each other's strengths?
  • Is there a genuine reason to act now?
  • Does each partner have sufficient capacity?
  • Are leadership teams aligned?

BEFORE STARTING

  • Are roles clear?
  • Are decision-making arrangements clear?
  • Are resources identified?
  • Is the government/community interface understood?
  • Are data responsibilities clear?
  • Are risks identified?

DURING IMPLEMENTATION

  • Are partners communicating regularly?
  • Are assumptions being tested?
  • Is feedback being collected?
  • Are disagreements being surfaced?
  • Are partners learning from implementation?
  • Is the approach being adapted where necessary?

AFTER THE INITIAL PHASE

  • What changed?
  • What did we learn?
  • What worked?
  • What did not work?
  • What should be changed?
  • Should the collaboration continue?
  • Should it expand?
  • Should it stop?
  • What institutional capacity remains after the collaboration?

From partnership to collective capability

The ultimate purpose of urban collaboration is not to create more partnerships.

It is to increase the capacity of a city's ecosystem to solve problems.

That distinction matters.

A partnership may end after a project.

But if the organisations involved have developed:

  • stronger relationships;
  • shared knowledge;
  • better understanding of the city;
  • institutional trust;
  • improved methods;
  • stronger government connections;
  • better community engagement;
  • and the ability to work across organisational boundaries,

then the collaboration has produced something that can outlast the original project.

That is collective capability.

Conclusion: Cities need a collaboration habit

India's cities face problems that cross administrative, organisational and disciplinary boundaries.

The answer will not always be a new programme or another institution.

Sometimes the missing ingredient is simply a better way for existing actors to work together.

A practical urban collaboration framework can help organisations move from:

meeting → partnership

partnership → joint work

joint work → learning

learning → adaptation

adaptation → stronger urban outcomes

The most important question is therefore not:

"Who should we collaborate with?"

It is:

"What can we accomplish together that we cannot accomplish as effectively apart?"

That question should come before the MoU, before the project plan and before the funding proposal.

And the Urban Collaboration Checklist provides a practical way to start answering it.

Frequently asked questions

What is an Urban Collaboration Checklist?

It is a practical framework for assessing whether different organisations or institutions should collaborate on an urban problem and, if so, how they should structure the partnership.

Who can use an Urban Collaboration Checklist?

Municipal governments, NGOs, urban practitioners, researchers, philanthropic organisations, community groups and other organisations involved in city development can use it.

Why do urban collaborations fail?

Common challenges include unclear objectives, insufficient trust, different organisational priorities, lack of staff capacity, unclear decision-making and inadequate resources. U-CAN's Request for Collaboration research also highlights organisational systems, funding structures, time and flexibility as important factors.

Does every urban problem require collaboration?

No. Collaboration is most useful when different actors possess complementary capabilities or when the problem crosses organisational or sectoral boundaries.

How can a city start a collaboration?

Start by defining the problem, identifying the desired outcome, mapping relevant stakeholders and assessing whether potential partners have complementary capabilities and sufficient readiness.

What is the most important question before starting a partnership?

A useful starting question is: Why do we need each other to solve this problem?

How should collaboration success be measured?

Measure more than meetings and activities. Look at whether partners developed useful joint work, learned from one another and contributed to measurable changes in the underlying urban problem.

About the author

U-CAN editorial team

U-CAN is a network of organisations working together to strengthen urban problem-solving in India's Tier II and Tier III cities. Formed in 2022 by a founding circle of twelve city-focused organisations, U-CAN today convenes practitioners, government officials, researchers and philanthropies around one shared goal: safer, more inclusive, better-governed cities for their residents.
