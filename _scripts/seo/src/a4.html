Municipal Government Reform in India: Building City Governments That Can Actually Govern

India's urban future depends not only on building better infrastructure, but on giving city governments the authority, resources, people and accountability needed to manage rapidly changing cities.

India's urban transformation has created an uncomfortable paradox.

Cities are becoming increasingly important to the country's economy, employment, infrastructure and quality of life. Yet the institutions responsible for managing many of these cities often operate within fragmented administrative, financial and institutional arrangements.

A city may have an elected municipal corporation, but important services may be controlled by state-level agencies.

A municipality may be held responsible by citizens for water, transport, sanitation or planning, while having limited control over the institutions delivering those services.

A municipal government may have ambitious development plans but insufficient revenue or specialised staff to implement them.

This is the central challenge of municipal government reform in India.

The issue is not simply whether municipalities need more money or whether cities need more infrastructure.

The deeper question is:

Can India's municipal governments become capable, accountable institutions of urban self-government?

Recent policy work suggests that this question is becoming increasingly difficult to postpone.

In April 2026, NITI Aayog published Moving Towards Effective City Government: A Framework for Million-Plus Cities. The report describes urban governance reform as a foundational requirement for India's economic, social and climate objectives and identifies challenges involving functional devolution, institutional fragmentation, municipal finances, leadership and capacity.

The debate has therefore moved beyond whether urban reform is necessary.

It is increasingly about what municipal reform should actually look like.

The constitutional foundation already exists

Municipal government reform in India did not begin with recent policy reports.

The constitutional foundation was created by the 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992.

The amendment gave constitutional recognition to municipalities as institutions of local self-government and established provisions relating to urban local bodies, their elections, reservations, finances and responsibilities.

The Twelfth Schedule identifies 18 functions that may be devolved to municipalities, including:

  • urban planning;
  • regulation of land use and construction;
  • planning for economic and social development;
  • roads and bridges;
  • water supply;
  • public health and sanitation;
  • solid waste management;
  • fire services;
  • urban forestry and environmental protection;
  • slum improvement;
  • urban poverty alleviation;
  • parks and public amenities; and
  • other urban functions.

But there is an important qualification.

The constitutional framework provides an enabling structure; the actual extent of devolution depends substantially on state legislation and administrative arrangements.

NITI Aayog's 2026 framework explicitly notes that the extent of devolution of powers, functions and financial autonomy varies across states.

That is why India's municipal reform conversation cannot be separated from the relationship between state governments and urban local governments.

The problem of responsibility without authority

One of the most persistent problems in urban governance is the mismatch between responsibility and authority.

Citizens generally experience a city as one system.

They expect water supply, roads, public transport, sanitation, waste management, drainage, parks and public spaces to work together.

Government structures, however, may divide these responsibilities among multiple organisations.

A municipal corporation may handle one function.

A development authority another.

A state department another.

A specialised parastatal another.

A special-purpose vehicle may manage a particular project.

The result can be a confusing accountability chain.

When something fails, citizens may know whom they want to complain to, but the institution receiving the complaint may not possess the authority to solve the problem.

NITI Aayog's 2026 framework identifies this fragmentation as a significant issue and recommends bringing certain essential urban services under effective city-government control.

This is fundamentally an accountability problem.

If an elected city government is expected to answer for urban outcomes, it needs meaningful control over the functions for which it is held accountable.

Municipal reform is not simply administrative reform

It is tempting to think of municipal reform as a technical exercise.

Change a municipal law.

Create a new department.

Introduce an online platform.

Recruit more engineers.

Modernise accounting.

All of these can matter.

But municipal government reform is ultimately about the architecture through which a city makes decisions.

A functioning city government requires at least five interconnected capabilities:

1. Political leadership

There must be clear democratic leadership capable of establishing priorities.

2. Administrative capacity

Municipalities need people with expertise in planning, engineering, finance, procurement, public health, law, technology and project management.

3. Functional authority

The institution responsible for an outcome needs meaningful control over the functions required to deliver it.

4. Financial capacity

Municipalities need predictable resources and credible mechanisms for raising and managing revenue.

5. Public accountability

Citizens need to know who is responsible and how decisions are made.

Weakness in any one of these areas can undermine the others.

The municipal finance problem

A city government cannot be genuinely autonomous if it has little ability to finance its responsibilities.

The Reserve Bank of India provides important evidence on this issue.

Its November 2024 Report on Municipal Finances analysed budgetary data from 232 municipal corporations, covering more than 90% of municipal corporations in the country. The RBI found that municipal corporations continued to rely heavily on transfers and grants from higher levels of government, while their own-source revenues were inadequate to meet revenue expenditure for most corporations.

This has major implications.

If municipalities depend heavily on transfers, their ability to plan independently can be constrained.

If their revenue base is weak, maintaining infrastructure becomes difficult.

If accounting and financial systems are inadequate, long-term financial planning becomes harder.

And if a municipality is expected to deliver more services without corresponding resources, the gap between expectations and capacity grows.

Municipal finance therefore cannot be treated as a technical accounting issue.

It is part of local democracy.

Property tax is one piece of the reform puzzle

Property tax is particularly important because it can provide municipalities with an own-source revenue stream.

The RBI's 2024 report highlights the potential for better property-tax administration through tools such as GIS mapping, digital payment systems, dynamic valuation systems and improved monitoring to reduce leakages.

This illustrates an important point about municipal reform.

Technology can improve municipal finance.

But technology is not the reform itself.

A GIS system can identify properties.

It cannot by itself determine whether the valuation framework is fair.

A digital payment platform can make collection easier.

It cannot by itself solve political or administrative resistance to revenue reforms.

Better municipal finance therefore requires a combination of:

better data + better systems + better administration + appropriate policy + public accountability.

State Finance Commissions matter

Municipal finances also depend on the broader system of fiscal transfers between levels of government.

The Constitution provides for State Finance Commissions to review the financial position of local governments and make recommendations concerning matters such as distribution of revenues and grants.

Yet the effectiveness of this mechanism depends on regular constitution, quality of recommendations and implementation.

NITI Aayog's 2026 city-government framework specifically recommends strengthening State Finance Commissions and improving the implementation of their recommendations as part of municipal fiscal reform.

This is an important reminder:

Municipal financial autonomy cannot be created entirely inside the municipal corporation.

It is also shaped by the fiscal architecture between states and cities.

Human resources may be the least visible municipal crisis

A city can have a master plan.

It can have a budget.

It can have a digital platform.

It can even have funding for a major project.

But without people capable of planning, procuring, managing and monitoring that work, implementation becomes difficult.

NITI Aayog has identified shortages in specialised municipal skills, including areas such as urban planning, engineering, finance and project management.

Earlier NITI Aayog work on modernising city governance similarly identified understaffing and skills gaps within urban local bodies as significant challenges.

This raises a basic question:

Can a municipality be expected to govern a rapidly growing city without a sufficiently capable professional workforce?

Municipal reform therefore needs a human-capital strategy.

That could involve:

  • better municipal recruitment;
  • specialist cadres;
  • continuous professional development;
  • shared technical-resource centres;
  • inter-municipal learning;
  • digital tools;
  • partnerships with universities and professional institutions; and
  • mechanisms that allow smaller municipalities to access specialised expertise.

Smaller cities cannot simply copy metropolitan models

India's municipal reform debate often focuses on the largest cities.

That is understandable.

But India's urban system is much broader.

A smaller city may not require the same institutional structure as Mumbai, Bengaluru or Delhi.

Its problems may instead involve rapid expansion, limited technical capacity, weak municipal revenue, inadequate planning systems and difficulty attracting specialised professionals.

This is why municipal reform should not become a one-size-fits-all exercise.

A reform that works in a million-plus city may need modification before it can work in a smaller municipality.

At the same time, smaller cities should not be excluded from institutional innovation.

They may benefit considerably from:

  • shared digital infrastructure;
  • common procurement platforms;
  • pooled technical expertise;
  • regional planning mechanisms;
  • peer-learning networks;
  • open-source municipal tools; and
  • partnerships with civil-society organisations and universities.

The objective should be appropriate institutional capacity, not simply larger bureaucracies.

Technology can modernise municipalities—but cannot replace governance

Digital transformation is now an important part of India's urban policy landscape.

The National Urban Digital Mission (NUDM), launched by the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs, seeks to build shared digital infrastructure for India's urban ecosystem and support accessible, inclusive, efficient and citizen-centric urban governance.

This can support municipalities through:

  • digital service delivery;
  • urban data platforms;
  • GIS;
  • property mapping;
  • digital payments;
  • grievance systems;
  • interoperable platforms; and
  • improved information management.

But there is a danger in assuming that digitisation automatically equals reform.

Putting a weak process online does not necessarily make the process better.

The better approach is:

simplify → redesign → digitise → measure.

Municipalities should first understand the problem and redesign the process around citizens and staff.

Technology should then make that improved process easier, faster and more transparent.

Municipal reform should strengthen accountability

A major purpose of municipal government is democratic accountability.

Citizens should be able to identify who is responsible for important urban decisions.

This becomes difficult when responsibilities are distributed across multiple organisations.

NITI Aayog's 2026 framework proposes a number of reforms intended to strengthen city-level accountability, including an empowered city government, clearer responsibility for essential urban services and changes to the relationship between municipal governments and state-controlled parastatals. These are policy recommendations, not changes that automatically apply across India.

The underlying principle is straightforward:

Authority, responsibility and accountability should be aligned as far as practical.

If a city government is responsible for a service, it should have sufficient authority to influence that service.

If another institution controls the service, citizens should know where responsibility actually lies.

Without this clarity, accountability becomes diffused.

The question of mayoral leadership

Leadership is another major component of India's municipal reform debate.

NITI Aayog's 2026 framework recommends, for million-plus cities, a model involving a directly elected Mayor with a fixed five-year term and an empowered Mayor-in-Council arrangement.

This is a policy recommendation, not a uniform constitutional requirement for all Indian municipalities.

Its importance lies in the debate it raises:

Who should have the authority to lead a city?

Supporters of stronger mayoral systems argue that clearer political leadership can make responsibility more visible and decision-making more coherent.

But institutional design involves trade-offs.

A mayoral system must also address checks and balances, administrative professionalism, councillor roles, state-government relationships and accountability.

Therefore, municipal reform should not be reduced to a debate about one office.

The larger question is how to construct a city government where leadership, administration and democratic oversight work together.

Ward-level governance can bring government closer to citizens

City government can become distant from residents when decisions are concentrated at the municipal headquarters.

This is why ward-level and neighbourhood-level institutions matter.

NITI Aayog's 2026 framework recommends stronger sub-city mechanisms, including mandatory Zonal Committees for large cities and continued use of Ward Committees and Area Sabhas as appropriate to state frameworks.

These institutions can potentially provide a bridge between:

citywide planning

and

neighbourhood-level priorities.

They can help elected representatives, municipal officials and citizens discuss issues such as:

  • local roads;
  • sanitation;
  • waste management;
  • parks;
  • drainage;
  • public spaces;
  • local infrastructure; and
  • service delivery.

The critical point, however, is that committees need meaningful functions and information.

Creating another committee without authority or resources does not automatically create decentralised governance.

Reform should focus on services citizens actually experience

Municipal reform can become overly focused on organisational charts.

But residents experience cities through services.

They care whether:

  • water arrives reliably;
  • garbage is collected;
  • streets are usable;
  • drainage works;
  • public spaces are accessible;
  • permits can be obtained without unnecessary delays;
  • property records are accurate;
  • complaints receive responses;
  • public transport is available;
  • neighbourhoods are safe and accessible.

Municipal reform should therefore connect institutional reform with measurable service outcomes.

A useful reform question is:

What institutional change would make this service more reliable for residents?

That question helps prevent reform from becoming an abstract administrative exercise.

Urban reform requires collaboration beyond government

Municipal governments cannot solve every urban challenge alone.

Universities can provide research.

Civil-society organisations can bring community knowledge.

Technology organisations can provide platforms and tools.

Professional organisations can provide technical expertise.

Philanthropic organisations can support experimentation and capacity building.

Other cities can provide lessons.

This creates an important role for urban learning networks.

Organisations such as the Urban Collective Action Network (U-CAN) illustrate one model of bringing together urban practitioners, government officials, researchers and philanthropies around urban problem-solving, particularly in Tier II and Tier III cities.

The value of such networks is not that they replace government.

They do not.

Their potential value is in helping governments and practitioners find, adapt and share knowledge that can strengthen local problem-solving.

For municipal reform, that learning function can be particularly valuable.

A city should not have to solve every problem from scratch.

What should municipal government reform in India prioritise?

A practical reform agenda can be organised around seven connected priorities.

1. Clarify responsibilities

Citizens and institutions should know which level of government is responsible for which urban function.

2. Match authority with responsibility

A municipality should have meaningful authority over functions for which it is held accountable.

3. Strengthen municipal finances

Improve own-source revenue, property-tax administration, financial management and predictable intergovernmental transfers.

4. Build professional capacity

Address shortages in planning, engineering, finance, procurement, technology and project management.

5. Strengthen democratic leadership

Ensure that municipal leadership has clear responsibilities and appropriate institutional checks and balances.

6. Bring decision-making closer to neighbourhoods

Strengthen ward-level and other participatory mechanisms where appropriate.

7. Measure reform through citizen outcomes

The ultimate question should be whether institutional reforms result in better urban services and improved experiences for residents.

A municipal reform checklist

Before introducing a new municipal reform, policymakers and urban organisations can ask:

Governance

  • Which institution is responsible?
  • Does it have the legal authority to act?

Finance

  • What will the reform cost?
  • What is the sustainable source of funding?

People

  • Does the municipality have the skills required to implement it?

Data

  • What information is needed?
  • Who owns and maintains it?

Citizens

  • How will residents experience the change?
  • How will vulnerable and digitally excluded groups be included?

Accountability

  • Who is answerable if implementation fails?

Coordination

  • Which other government agencies must cooperate?

Measurement

  • What measurable outcome will demonstrate progress?

Learning

  • Can the reform be adapted by another municipality?

These questions can turn municipal reform from a policy announcement into an implementation framework.

The real reform is institutional, not cosmetic

India does not lack urban policy initiatives.

There are major programmes for water, sanitation, housing, transport, digital governance, infrastructure and urban transformation.

The harder challenge is ensuring that the institutions responsible for implementing these programmes can coordinate, finance, manage and sustain them.

This is why municipal government reform deserves to be treated as an issue in its own right.

A city cannot become resilient simply because it has a climate plan.

It cannot become inclusive simply because it has a citizen portal.

It cannot become financially sustainable simply because it collects property tax digitally.

And it cannot become accountable simply because it has an elected municipal body.

The institutions have to work.

They need authority.

They need resources.

They need people.

They need information.

They need accountability.

And they need the ability to coordinate.

The next phase of India's urbanisation requires stronger city governments

India's cities are becoming too economically important, too environmentally vulnerable and too complex to be governed through fragmented responsibility alone.

The 74th Constitutional Amendment created the foundation for urban local self-government.

Three decades later, the question is how to make that constitutional vision function effectively in practice.

The latest NITI Aayog framework places this question squarely on the policy agenda. Its recommendations include stronger city-level leadership, clearer functional responsibility, greater municipal fiscal capacity, stronger State Finance Commissions and closer alignment between city governments and agencies delivering essential urban services.

The RBI's municipal-finance analysis reinforces another part of the picture: without stronger own-source revenues and better municipal financial systems, local governments may struggle to sustain their responsibilities.

Neither reform nor urbanisation will happen in exactly the same way in every state.

India's federal structure means that state governments have a central role in determining how municipalities are empowered.

That makes municipal reform a long-term institutional process rather than a single national policy announcement.

The objective should not simply be to create bigger municipalities.

It should be to create more capable, accountable and responsive city governments.

Because India's urban future will ultimately depend not only on what cities build—but on how effectively they govern what they build.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is municipal government reform in India?

Municipal government reform refers to changes intended to improve the structure, powers, finances, administrative capacity, accountability and service-delivery capabilities of urban local governments.

Why is the 74th Constitutional Amendment important?

The 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992 established the constitutional framework for urban local self-government and identified 18 functions in the Twelfth Schedule for potential devolution to municipalities.

What are the major challenges facing municipal governments in India?

Challenges include fragmented responsibilities, uneven devolution of functions, limited financial autonomy, inadequate staffing and specialised skills, overlapping institutions and difficulties coordinating citywide services. NITI Aayog's 2026 framework identifies these as important elements of the urban governance reform agenda.

Why is municipal finance important?

Municipal governments need sustainable revenue to operate services, maintain infrastructure and plan long-term investments. RBI's 2024 analysis found that most municipal corporations' own-source revenues were inadequate to meet revenue expenditure and that they remained heavily reliant on transfers and grants.

What is the role of State Finance Commissions?

State Finance Commissions are constitutionally established mechanisms for reviewing the financial position of local governments and recommending arrangements relating to revenue distribution, transfers and grants. Their timely constitution and effective implementation are important components of municipal fiscal decentralisation.

Can technology solve municipal governance problems?

Technology can improve information, service delivery, revenue administration, transparency and citizen interaction, but it cannot substitute for institutional authority, skilled staff, sound processes and accountable decision-making.

Why does municipal reform matter for smaller cities?

Smaller and emerging cities face rapid urbanisation but may have fewer technical and financial resources than large metropolitan governments. Strengthening municipal capacity, shared services and inter-city learning can therefore be particularly important.
