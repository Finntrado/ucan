Inclusive Urban Governance in India: Who Gets a Voice in the City?

A city cannot be called inclusive simply because everyone is allowed to live in it. Inclusion depends on whether different groups can access services, public spaces, economic opportunities and the institutions that shape urban decisions.

When a city prepares a development plan, who participates?

When a new road is designed, whose daily journey is considered?

When public transport routes are decided, are the experiences of women, older people, workers and persons with disabilities understood?

When a neighbourhood is redeveloped, what happens to people whose livelihoods depend on informal economic activity?

And when a municipality asks citizens for feedback, who actually has the time, information and confidence to respond?

These questions lead to a larger issue:

What does inclusive urban governance actually mean?

India's urbanisation is creating new economic opportunities, but it is also exposing differences in access to housing, infrastructure, mobility, public services, digital systems and decision-making.

For this reason, inclusive urban governance cannot simply mean building more infrastructure and making services available.

It means making urban institutions and decision-making processes capable of recognising different needs, different experiences and different forms of exclusion.

Inclusion begins with a simple question: who is missing?

Urban planning often works with categories such as population, households, land use, infrastructure demand and economic activity.

These are essential.

But they do not always reveal who is being left behind.

A citywide transport plan may show routes and passenger volumes.

It may not reveal why a woman avoids a particular route after sunset.

A housing programme may count units delivered.

It may not show whether the people most in need can actually afford to live there.

A digital municipal service may be technically available to everyone.

It may still be difficult for residents with limited digital literacy, unreliable connectivity or disabilities to use.

A public space may be officially open to everyone.

Its design, location, lighting, accessibility or social environment may nevertheless discourage particular groups from using it.

This is why inclusion requires more than equal treatment.

It requires understanding whether different groups can actually access and benefit from the city.

What is inclusive urban governance?

Inclusive urban governance can be understood as a system in which people from different social and economic circumstances have meaningful opportunities to participate in urban life and in decisions affecting their communities.

It has several dimensions.

Political inclusion

Residents should have meaningful opportunities to participate in local governance and hold institutions accountable.

Social inclusion

Urban policies should consider groups that experience barriers because of income, gender, disability, age, migration status or other circumstances.

Economic inclusion

Urban development should expand access to employment, markets, livelihoods and economic opportunities.

Spatial inclusion

Infrastructure, housing, transport and public facilities should not systematically exclude particular neighbourhoods or communities.

Digital inclusion

Technology-enabled services should not create new barriers for people who lack devices, connectivity, accessibility or digital skills.

Institutional inclusion

The voices of affected communities should have appropriate channels to influence planning and implementation.

These dimensions overlap.

A person who cannot afford housing near employment may also face longer commutes.

A person with a disability may face barriers in transport, employment and public buildings simultaneously.

A street vendor may have a livelihood that depends on access to public space but limited influence over how that space is regulated.

Inclusive governance therefore requires cities to understand interconnected forms of exclusion, rather than treating each issue as an isolated problem.

Inclusion is not the same as consultation

One of the most common misunderstandings about inclusive governance is that consultation automatically makes a decision inclusive.

It does not.

A public meeting may be open to everyone, but not everyone can attend.

A notice may be publicly available, but not everyone may understand the technical language.

An online consultation may appear accessible, but it excludes people without reliable internet access.

A hearing during working hours may be difficult for daily-wage workers to attend.

A planning document may be technically public but practically inaccessible.

Therefore, the question should not simply be:

“Did we consult citizens?”

It should be:

“Who was able to participate, who was not, and how did their participation influence the process?”

That is a much more demanding standard.

The neighbourhood is where inclusion becomes visible

Urban governance can appear highly participatory at the city level while remaining distant from everyday neighbourhood problems.

Consider a municipal budget.

At the city level, it may contain allocations for roads, parks, sanitation and public infrastructure.

But residents experience those decisions at a much smaller scale.

Which road gets repaired?

Where is the bus stop?

Is the local park accessible?

Does drainage work?

Where can children safely play?

Is there adequate lighting?

Can a person using a wheelchair reach the public building?

Can a street vendor operate without constant uncertainty?

These are neighbourhood-level questions.

This is why decentralised institutions can play an important role in inclusive urban governance.

NITI Aayog's 2026 Moving Towards Effective City Government framework recommends mandatory Zonal Committees for million-plus cities and identifies Ward Committees and Area Sabhas as potential mechanisms for increasing citizen participation at grassroots levels, subject to state-level arrangements.

The principle is important:

The closer decisions are to the people affected by them, the greater the opportunity to understand local priorities.

But decentralisation only works when these forums have meaningful functions, information and institutional support.

A committee that meets without influence over decisions is unlikely to produce meaningful inclusion.

Women experience cities differently

Gender is one of the clearest examples of why inclusive urban governance needs to look beyond population statistics.

The World Bank's Handbook for Gender-Inclusive Urban Planning and Design notes that conventional urban environments have often reflected the needs and travel patterns of men more strongly than those of women and other groups. It recommends participatory approaches that incorporate the experiences of women, girls and gender minorities into planning and design.

Consider mobility.

A conventional transport analysis might focus on:

  • travel time;
  • passenger volumes;
  • route efficiency;
  • vehicle capacity.

A gender-inclusive analysis may additionally ask:

  • Who is travelling?
  • At what times?
  • For what purposes?
  • Are trips chained around childcare or household responsibilities?
  • Are stops accessible and well connected?
  • Is the surrounding public space usable after dark?
  • Are there safe and practical options for the first and last kilometre?

The difference is not cosmetic.

It changes what planners consider a successful transport system.

Disability inclusion must begin with the built environment

Inclusive urban governance also requires accessibility to be treated as a fundamental planning consideration.

India's Ministry of Urban Development's Harmonised Guidelines and Space Standards for Barrier-Free Built Environment for Persons with Disabilities and Elderly Persons were developed to guide accessible design in the physical environment, transportation, information and communication. The guidelines emphasise equitable access and encourage their adoption by state and urban planning institutions.

This matters because accessibility is often addressed after a project has already been designed.

A ramp is added.

A tactile path is retrofitted.

An accessible toilet is squeezed into an existing layout.

But universal accessibility is more effective when considered from the beginning.

The question should therefore be:

Can people with different physical, sensory and cognitive needs use this space independently and safely?

That should be part of the design brief—not an afterthought.

Informal workers are part of the urban economy

Another major test of inclusive urban governance concerns informal workers.

Street vendors, domestic workers, waste pickers, construction workers, small traders and other informal workers contribute significantly to urban economies.

Yet their relationship with urban planning can be complicated.

A city may want cleaner streets.

A market may need better pedestrian movement.

A transport corridor may need redesign.

A redevelopment project may seek to change land use.

Each objective can be legitimate.

But the question is whether affected livelihoods are considered as part of the planning process.

Inclusive governance does not mean that every existing activity must remain unchanged.

It means that decisions affecting livelihoods should recognise the people involved, assess the consequences and provide appropriate mechanisms for participation, transition or mitigation where required.

This is particularly important because urban policy can unintentionally transfer costs from the city system to people with the least capacity to absorb them.

Housing inclusion is more than building houses

Housing is another area where the difference between physical provision and meaningful inclusion becomes clear.

A housing unit may be affordable according to a programme's eligibility criteria.

But affordability depends on the total cost of living.

A household must also consider:

  • transport;
  • utilities;
  • maintenance;
  • access to schools;
  • access to employment;
  • healthcare;
  • childcare;
  • commuting time.

A house far from employment can impose substantial transport and time costs.

A housing development without nearby services can create another form of exclusion.

This is why inclusive urban housing needs to be considered alongside location, connectivity, livelihoods and access to services.

The city should not be designed only for the average resident

Urban planning often relies on averages.

Average household size.

Average travel time.

Average income.

Average demand.

Average population density.

But cities are not inhabited by average people.

They are inhabited by individuals with different routines, resources and constraints.

A person working remotely may have a very different mobility pattern from a construction worker.

A school-going child experiences streets differently from a delivery worker.

An older person may value proximity and seating more than speed.

A wheelchair user may evaluate a footpath through completely different criteria.

A woman travelling with children may have different mobility needs from a single commuter.

Inclusive urban governance therefore asks planners to look beyond the average user.

The objective is not to design a separate city for every group.

It is to design systems that accommodate meaningful differences without creating unnecessary barriers.

Digital inclusion is now part of urban inclusion

As municipal services become increasingly digital, access to technology is becoming an urban governance issue.

India's National Urban Digital Mission seeks to create shared digital infrastructure for urban governance and explicitly promotes accessible, inclusive and citizen-centric services.

But digital inclusion requires more than internet connectivity.

It includes:

  • device access;
  • affordability;
  • digital literacy;
  • language;
  • accessibility for persons with disabilities;
  • ease of navigation;
  • assisted access;
  • trust and privacy.

A municipal service that requires five complicated online steps may technically be available to everyone.

It may not be practically accessible to everyone.

This is why digital public services should be designed around different users rather than around the capabilities of the technology itself.

Data can reveal exclusion—but only if cities ask the right questions

Urban data is becoming increasingly important for planning.

But aggregate data can conceal inequality.

Suppose a city reports 95% household access to a particular service.

That statistic may look impressive.

But what if access is significantly lower in certain neighbourhoods?

What if informal settlements are poorly represented?

What if women and men experience the service differently?

What if people with disabilities encounter physical barriers despite nominal access?

The solution is not to abandon aggregate indicators.

It is to disaggregate and interpret them carefully.

Cities can examine data by:

  • neighbourhood;
  • income;
  • gender;
  • age;
  • disability;
  • type of settlement;
  • service availability;
  • distance and accessibility.

The objective is not to collect data for its own sake.

It is to identify where apparently successful citywide outcomes conceal local exclusion.

Participation requires information

People cannot participate meaningfully in decisions they cannot understand.

This is particularly important in technical urban planning.

Master plans can contain complex maps, land-use categories, development regulations and infrastructure proposals.

For meaningful public participation, residents need information translated into practical questions:

What changes in my neighbourhood?

What happens to the road outside my home?

Where will public facilities be located?

What happens to existing livelihoods?

How will transport change?

Which areas are protected?

Who pays for the infrastructure?

When will implementation happen?

This does not mean simplifying away important technical information.

It means presenting it in forms that enable wider participation.

Inclusive governance requires institutions, not only goodwill

Individual officials and community leaders can make enormous differences.

But inclusion cannot depend entirely on individual goodwill.

It needs institutional mechanisms.

These may include:

  • functioning Ward Committees;
  • Area Sabhas where provided for;
  • public consultations;
  • accessible planning information;
  • grievance systems;
  • social audits or public monitoring mechanisms where appropriate;
  • participatory budgeting approaches where suitable;
  • citizen charters;
  • transparent municipal information;
  • accessibility standards;
  • gender-responsive planning processes;
  • community engagement frameworks.

The institutionalisation of participation matters because people and governments change.

A good system should survive the departure of an individual champion.

Inclusive governance also means being honest about trade-offs

Urban decisions rarely have only winners.

A road expansion may improve traffic flow but affect businesses or homes.

A pedestrianisation project may improve public space but require changes in vehicle access.

A redevelopment project may improve infrastructure but disrupt existing livelihoods.

A conservation measure may restrict certain forms of land use.

An inclusive governance process should not pretend these trade-offs do not exist.

Instead, it should make them visible.

Citizens should be able to understand:

  • what is being proposed;
  • why it is being proposed;
  • who may benefit;
  • who may be affected;
  • what alternatives were considered;
  • what safeguards exist;
  • how implementation will be monitored.

Inclusion is not the promise that everyone will get everything they want.

It is the commitment that people affected by important decisions will not simply be invisible within the decision-making process.

Amaravati illustrates the importance of building inclusion into city institutions

The development of Amaravati provides a contemporary example of how inclusive governance can be incorporated into the institutional design of a growing city.

The World Bank's Amaravati programme is intended to strengthen institutions and infrastructure planning for inclusive and sustainable urban growth. Its design includes citizen engagement and participation in city planning and development, with particular attention to women, low-income groups, minorities and vulnerable groups.

The lesson is broader than Amaravati.

When inclusion is incorporated into governance arrangements from the beginning, it has the potential to influence not just individual projects but the way institutions operate.

That is different from adding consultation after a plan has already been finalised.

Inclusive cities need inclusive institutions

A city can have inclusive policies and still have exclusionary institutions.

Consider a municipality where:

  • planning information is technically public but difficult to access;
  • public meetings are held but poorly attended;
  • digital services exclude people with limited digital literacy;
  • ward-level bodies exist but rarely influence decisions;
  • accessibility is addressed only after construction;
  • low-income neighbourhoods have limited representation.

The policy documents may use the language of inclusion.

The institutional experience may tell a different story.

This is why inclusive urban governance should be evaluated through institutional practice, not simply policy language.

A practical framework for inclusive urban governance

Cities and organisations working on urban development can use six questions as a starting point.

1. Representation

Who is affected by this decision?

Identify groups that may experience the policy differently.

2. Access

Can all relevant groups access the service, space or opportunity?

Look beyond nominal availability.

3. Voice

Can affected people meaningfully participate before the decision is finalised?

4. Influence

Can participation actually change priorities, design or implementation?

5. Accountability

Can citizens understand who is responsible and what happened after participation?

6. Outcomes

Did the intervention reduce the identified barrier?

This framework shifts inclusion from a statement of intent to something that can be examined.

An inclusive urban governance checklist

Before launching an urban policy, programme or project, decision-makers can ask:

People

  • Who will be affected?

Representation

  • Whose voice is missing from the planning process?

Accessibility

  • Can people with disabilities access the proposed service or space?

Gender

  • Could women and men experience the intervention differently?

Affordability

  • Could the intervention create additional financial burdens?

Livelihoods

  • Could informal or low-income workers be affected?

Digital access

  • Does the service assume universal digital access?

Neighbourhoods

  • Are impacts different across parts of the city?

Participation

  • Can citizens participate before important decisions are finalised?

Influence

  • What mechanism exists for their inputs to affect the outcome?

Transparency

  • Will government explain the final decision and its rationale?

Measurement

  • How will inclusion be evaluated after implementation?

From inclusive projects to inclusive systems

The biggest challenge is moving from individual inclusive projects to inclusive urban systems.

A municipality might successfully make one park accessible.

That is useful.

But what about the footpath leading to it?

What about the public transport system?

What about the municipal building?

What about the school?

What about the digital service used to report maintenance problems?

A truly inclusive city requires these systems to reinforce one another.

The same principle applies to gender.

A women-friendly bus stop is valuable.

But if the route does not connect to employment centres, if surrounding streets are inaccessible or if the service does not operate when women need it, the intervention has limited effect.

Inclusion therefore has to travel across systems.

Why inclusive governance matters for smaller Indian cities

The inclusion challenge is not restricted to India's largest metropolitan regions.

Smaller and emerging cities are experiencing significant changes in population, employment, infrastructure and land use.

Their institutional capacities can vary substantially.

In such settings, inclusive governance can benefit from collaboration.

Local organisations may understand communities deeply.

Municipal officials understand administrative systems.

Researchers can contribute evidence.

Urban practitioners can provide implementation experience.

Technology organisations can provide tools.

State governments can provide institutional and financial support.

Networks can connect experiences across cities.

The objective should not be to impose one model on every city.

It should be to create mechanisms through which cities can learn how to identify and address exclusion in their own context.

The role of urban networks and collective learning

Inclusive governance is difficult partly because cities often face similar problems without sharing enough practical knowledge.

One municipality may have developed a useful approach to community participation.

Another may have improved accessibility.

A third may have developed better neighbourhood-level engagement.

A fourth may have created a more effective mechanism for collaboration between officials and civil-society organisations.

The opportunity is to turn these individual experiences into shared learning.

Urban networks can contribute by:

  • connecting practitioners;
  • documenting experiences;
  • creating peer-learning spaces;
  • bringing government and civil society into dialogue;
  • identifying transferable approaches;
  • supporting experimentation;
  • communicating lessons across cities.

For networks such as the Urban Collective Action Network (U-CAN), this creates a potentially useful role in strengthening the ecosystem around urban problem-solving in emerging Indian cities.

The network itself does not replace government.

Its potential contribution is to help different actors learn, connect and collaborate around problems that are difficult for one institution to solve alone.

Measuring inclusion: what should cities look for?

A city cannot improve what it never examines.

But measuring inclusion requires more than counting consultations.

Useful indicators can include:

Participation

Who participated in planning and decision-making?

Representation

Did affected groups have a meaningful presence?

Accessibility

Can people with disabilities use the relevant infrastructure and services?

Service equity

Are service levels comparable across different neighbourhoods?

Affordability

Can lower-income households realistically access the service?

Digital inclusion

Can people access services through appropriate channels?

Responsiveness

Are complaints and requests addressed within defined timelines?

Outcome equity

Did the intervention improve outcomes for groups previously experiencing disadvantage?

These indicators should be adapted to the specific policy or city rather than treated as a universal scorecard.

Inclusion is ultimately about power

At its deepest level, inclusive urban governance is not simply about better infrastructure.

It is about who has the ability to influence decisions that shape urban life.

Who decides where infrastructure is built?

Who decides which neighbourhood receives investment first?

Who determines how public space is used?

Who defines what counts as a priority?

Who has access to information?

Who can challenge a decision?

Who is heard when competing interests disagree?

These are governance questions.

And they cannot be solved simply through technology or construction.

A city becomes more inclusive when its institutions become better at recognising different interests, making information accessible, enabling meaningful participation and explaining decisions.

The goal is not a perfect city

No city can eliminate every inequality.

Resources are limited.

Interests conflict.

Urban populations change.

Climate risks evolve.

Technology creates new opportunities and new forms of exclusion.

Governments must constantly make choices.

The objective of inclusive urban governance is therefore not to create a city without disagreement.

It is to create a city where disagreement can be expressed, evidence can be considered, affected people can participate and institutions remain accountable.

That is a much more realistic—and more useful—definition of inclusion.

India's inclusive urban future will depend on who gets to shape it

India's urban transformation will create enormous physical and economic change.

But the quality of that transformation will depend partly on whether the institutions managing it can recognise the diversity of people who live and work in Indian cities.

The country's policy architecture already contains important building blocks.

The constitutional framework recognises urban local self-government.

NITI Aayog's current city-government framework highlights decentralised and participatory mechanisms.

Government accessibility guidelines recognise the need to remove barriers for persons with disabilities and older people.

Digital-government initiatives recognise the importance of accessible and inclusive services.

International urban-development work increasingly treats participation, accessibility and representation as components of sustainable urban development.

The remaining challenge is implementation.

Inclusive urban governance must move from:

consultation to participation,

from:

participation to influence,

from:

individual projects to institutional practice,

and from:

formal equality to meaningful access.

The most important question for India's cities may therefore not be:

“Are our cities inclusive?”

It may be:

“Who is still unable to fully participate in the city—and what can urban institutions change to remove that barrier?”

Finding credible answers to that question is the beginning of inclusive urban governance.

And ultimately, an inclusive city is not one where every person receives exactly the same thing.

It is one where different people have a genuine opportunity to access the city, participate in its future and live with dignity within it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is inclusive urban governance?

Inclusive urban governance is an approach to city governance in which different groups have meaningful opportunities to access urban services, participate in decisions and benefit from urban development. It considers social, economic, spatial, political, physical and digital barriers.

Why is inclusive urban governance important in India?

India's cities contain populations with very different incomes, occupations, mobility patterns, abilities and access to services. Governance systems that do not account for these differences can unintentionally reproduce exclusion.

Is citizen participation the same as inclusive governance?

No. Citizen participation is one component of inclusive governance. Inclusion also involves accessibility, affordability, representation, service equity, institutional accountability and the ability of affected groups to influence decisions.

How can women be included in urban planning?

Cities can incorporate women's experiences into planning for transport, public spaces, housing, employment access and safety. The World Bank's gender-inclusive planning guidance recommends participatory processes that actively incorporate the experiences of women, girls and gender minorities.

How does accessibility contribute to inclusive cities?

Accessible buildings, streets, transport systems, information and public spaces allow persons with disabilities and older people to participate more fully in urban life. India's Harmonised Guidelines provide standards and guidance for creating barrier-free built environments.

What role do Ward Committees and Area Sabhas play?

They can provide mechanisms for decentralised participation closer to neighbourhoods. NITI Aayog's 2026 framework recommends stronger sub-city governance arrangements, including Zonal Committees for million-plus cities and Ward Committees or Area Sabhas as determined by states.

Does inclusive urban governance mean every citizen gets the same outcome?

No. Inclusive governance is concerned with equitable access and meaningful participation, not necessarily identical outcomes. Different groups may require different forms of support or accommodation to participate equally.

How can smaller cities improve inclusive governance?

They can strengthen local participation mechanisms, improve accessibility, use appropriate digital and non-digital service channels, build municipal capacity and participate in peer-learning networks that allow practitioners to exchange practical experience.
