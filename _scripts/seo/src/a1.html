Sustainable Urban Development in India: Why the Next Urban Transition Must Be About Governance, Not Just Infrastructure

India’s urban future will not be secured by building more roads, housing and infrastructure alone. The harder challenge is creating cities that can govern growth, withstand climate risks, include citizens and coordinate across institutions.

India's urban story is entering a decisive phase.

The country is not simply adding more people to existing metropolitan centres. Urban growth is spreading across a much wider geography—from large cities and their peripheries to smaller towns and emerging urban settlements. The World Bank estimates that India's urban population could almost double from about 480 million in 2020 to 951 million by 2050. It also estimates that 70% of new jobs in India by 2030 are expected to come from cities.

Those numbers make urban development one of India's most important economic and social questions.

But they also expose a fundamental misconception: sustainable urban development is not simply an infrastructure challenge. It is a governance challenge.

Building housing without reliable water and sanitation does not create a sustainable neighbourhood. Expanding roads without considering public transport, pedestrians and drainage does not create a resilient city. Digitising municipal services without improving institutional capacity does not automatically create better governance.

The real question, therefore, is not merely how much India should invest in its cities.

It is how India can ensure that its cities have the institutions, capabilities, data, finance and collaborative mechanisms needed to use that investment well.

The urban challenge is bigger than the megacities

India's urban policy conversation has historically been dominated by its largest metropolitan areas. Yet a substantial part of the country's urban transformation is happening outside the metros.

Research presented by the World Resources Institute has highlighted the complexity of India's urban system, which ranges from small towns to megacities and includes statutory towns, census towns and rapidly urbanising peri-urban areas. It also points to the consequences of infrastructure deficits, inequality and fragmented approaches to urbanisation.

This matters because the governance problems of a smaller city are not necessarily smaller versions of those in Delhi, Mumbai or Bengaluru.

A Tier II or Tier III city may face a very different combination of challenges: limited technical capacity, inadequate urban data, weak planning systems, pressure on municipal finances, rapid peripheral growth, insufficient public transport and a shortage of specialised professionals.

Yet these cities are increasingly important to India's economic and social future.

A sustainable urban development strategy must therefore ask a different question:

How do we build institutional capacity in the places where urbanisation is happening next?

Sustainability begins with the systems behind the city

Urban sustainability is often discussed through familiar categories: renewable energy, green buildings, public transport, waste management, water conservation and climate resilience.

All of these matter.

But they are ultimately dependent on something less visible: the systems that make decisions.

Consider urban water.

A city can build treatment plants, pipelines and drainage infrastructure. But sustainable water management also requires planning authorities, municipal bodies, environmental agencies and communities to work from compatible information and priorities.

The same applies to urban rivers.

The Government of India's River Cities Alliance, led by the Ministry of Jal Shakti and the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs, has developed a platform for capacity building, knowledge exchange and river-sensitive urban planning. By April 2025, the alliance reported 145 member cities.

In June 2026, the National Mission for Clean Ganga reported that Urban River Management Plans had been completed for 13 cities, with additional plans being prepared across the Ganga Basin.

The significance goes beyond rivers.

It illustrates a broader principle of sustainable urban development: complex urban problems rarely respect administrative boundaries.

Water does not stop at a municipal boundary. Air pollution does not follow ward maps. Flooding can originate outside the area where its impacts are felt. Commuters cross jurisdictions every day. Waste, employment, housing and transport systems frequently operate at metropolitan or regional scales.

Urban governance must therefore become better at working across boundaries.

The climate question makes better urban governance urgent

Climate change is turning weaknesses in urban systems into increasingly visible economic and human risks.

The World Bank's 2025 assessment of India's cities notes that urban heat-island effects can make city centres more than 3–4°C hotter than surrounding areas. It also points to the growing flood risk associated with rapid built-up expansion and declining capacity of urban land to absorb stormwater.

These are not problems that can be solved by a single department.

A heat-resilient city may require better building design, tree cover, public spaces, health preparedness, worker protection, early-warning systems and neighbourhood-level interventions.

Flood resilience may require drainage improvements—but also watershed management, land-use planning, protection of water bodies and better enforcement.

This is why sustainable urban development must be treated as a systems problem.

The objective is not to create isolated examples of green infrastructure. It is to create urban systems capable of continuously identifying problems, coordinating responses, learning from experience and adapting to changing conditions.

The unfinished governance question

India's constitutional framework already recognises the importance of urban local government.

The Twelfth Schedule associated with the 74th Constitutional Amendment includes functions ranging from urban planning and land-use regulation to water supply, sanitation, solid waste management, urban forestry, slum improvement, poverty alleviation, parks and public amenities.

But listing responsibilities is not the same as ensuring that institutions have the practical capacity to exercise them effectively.

Urban reform therefore cannot be reduced to transferring functions on paper.

It must also involve questions of:

  • Who has decision-making authority?
  • Who has access to reliable urban data?
  • Who has the technical expertise to interpret that data?
  • Who controls or influences urban finance?
  • Which agencies are responsible for implementation?
  • How are citizens involved?
  • How are outcomes measured?
  • What happens when a problem crosses administrative boundaries?

These questions may sound administrative.

In reality, they determine whether an urban policy succeeds on the ground.

Digital governance can help—but technology is not the reform

India has already recognised the importance of digital infrastructure in urban governance.

The Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs launched the National Urban Digital Mission (NUDM) in 2021 with the objective of creating a shared digital infrastructure for India's urban ecosystem. Its stated vision is accessible, inclusive, efficient and citizen-centric governance across India's towns and cities.

This creates significant possibilities.

Better urban data can help governments understand service gaps. Digital platforms can simplify citizen interaction with municipalities. Shared standards can reduce duplication. Open and interoperable systems can allow different stakeholders to work with common infrastructure.

But digitisation alone cannot solve institutional weaknesses.

A badly designed process does not become a good process merely because it moves online.

The challenge is therefore to combine technology with institutional reform, human capability and citizen participation.

Citizens cannot remain consumers of urban services

A truly inclusive city is not one where citizens merely receive services.

Citizens are also sources of knowledge.

Residents know where flooding occurs after heavy rain. Street vendors understand how public-space regulations affect livelihoods. Women often experience public transport and public spaces differently from men. Migrant workers can identify barriers that are invisible in administrative datasets. Older people and people with disabilities can reveal accessibility failures that standard infrastructure audits may miss.

This is why citizen participation should not be treated as a ceremonial consultation exercise.

The more difficult question is how citizen knowledge can become part of the way cities make decisions.

That requires institutional mechanisms—not simply invitations to attend meetings.

Collaboration may be the missing urban infrastructure

There is another layer to India's urban challenge that receives less attention: the fragmentation of knowledge and action.

Across the country, civil-society organisations, researchers, urban practitioners, technology organisations, philanthropies and government institutions are working on different pieces of the urban puzzle.

One organisation may understand public transport.

Another may work on sanitation.

Another may specialise in citizen participation.

Another may work with municipal governments.

Another may have deep experience with climate resilience.

The problem is not necessarily a shortage of ideas.

It is often the difficulty of connecting the right ideas, people and institutions at the right time.

This is where collective action for urban governance becomes important.

An emerging model: learning across cities

The Urban Collective Action Network (U-CAN) provides one example of an organisation attempting to build such connective infrastructure.

U-CAN describes itself as a network working to strengthen urban problem-solving in India's Tier II and Tier III cities. It brings together practitioners, government officials, researchers and philanthropies around urban governance and liveability.

Its approach is built around three broad functions: Convene, Champion and Communicate.

The logic is straightforward but important.

First, practitioners need places where they can exchange experiences and share learnings.

Second, successful approaches need pathways through which they can influence broader urban reform.

Third, urban challenges need to be communicated in a way that attracts the political, institutional and financial attention necessary to address them.

According to U-CAN's current reporting, its network includes eight member organisations, more than 500 practitioners and work spanning more than 25 cities. It also reports engagement with more than 200 government officials through its Communities of Learning.

These figures should not be interpreted as evidence that a network alone can transform urban governance.

The more useful lesson is different.

Urban transformation may depend partly on creating institutions and networks that allow people solving similar problems in different places to learn from one another.

From individual projects to collective urban reform

This idea has gained a more explicit institutional form through U-CAN's Urban Reforms Collective.

The network reported the formal launch of the collective in Mumbai in June 2026, bringing together organisations working across different aspects of urban design, planning and governance. Its stated premise is that persistent problems such as institutional weakness, centralised decision-making and overstretched services can have deeper governance and policy causes that cannot be addressed by one organisation acting alone.

Whether such collective models ultimately produce durable reforms will depend on evidence over time.

But the direction is significant.

It shifts the conversation from:

“What project can my organisation implement?”

to:

“What system needs to change, and who needs to work together to change it?”

That may be one of the most important questions for the next phase of India's urbanisation.

What would a genuinely sustainable Indian city look like?

There is no single template.

A coastal city will face different risks from an inland city. A rapidly growing industrial town will have different priorities from a tourism-dependent city. A city surrounded by agricultural land will have different peri-urban pressures from a metropolitan region.

But some common principles can guide sustainable urban development in India.

1. Plan for growth before it becomes a crisis

Urban planning must anticipate population, employment, housing, transport, water and climate pressures rather than continually responding after infrastructure becomes inadequate.

2. Strengthen local institutions

Municipal governments need appropriate authority, financial capacity, technical expertise and reliable data to perform their responsibilities effectively.

3. Make climate resilience part of everyday planning

Climate resilience should not be a separate environmental project. It should influence land use, infrastructure, housing, transport, drainage, public spaces and public health.

4. Make digital systems interoperable and citizen-centred

Technology should reduce friction between citizens and government while strengthening transparency, accountability and evidence-based decision-making.

5. Build genuine citizen participation

Residents should contribute knowledge and help shape priorities, especially when policies directly affect livelihoods, mobility, housing and public space.

6. Learn across cities

Cities should not repeatedly reinvent solutions that have already been tested elsewhere.

Structured peer learning, Communities of Learning, practitioner networks and cross-city collaboration can shorten the distance between innovation and adoption.

7. Measure outcomes, not announcements

A new project is an input.

A functioning service is an outcome.

The ultimate test should be whether residents experience safer streets, cleaner water, better mobility, more reliable services, healthier environments and greater economic opportunity.

A practical collaboration checklist for cities

For governments, NGOs, researchers, philanthropies and urban practitioners considering a new urban initiative, five questions can be particularly useful:

1. What problem are we actually trying to solve?

Define the resident-level problem before defining the project.

2. Who already has knowledge or experience relevant to it?

Look beyond organisational boundaries.

3. Which institution has the authority to act?

Good ideas need an implementation pathway.

4. What evidence will tell us whether the intervention works?

Define measurable outcomes before announcing success.

5. What could be learned and transferred to another city?

A local solution becomes much more valuable when its lessons can travel.

India's urban future will be built through relationships

The conventional image of urban development is physical: roads, bridges, housing, metro systems, sewage networks, parks and buildings.

All are essential.

But beneath that physical infrastructure sits another infrastructure that is much harder to see: institutions, trust, knowledge, data, relationships and the ability to coordinate.

India's urban population is expected to grow dramatically in the coming decades. The country therefore has a rare opportunity to make choices before future urban form becomes prohibitively expensive to change. The World Bank's assessment makes the same basic point: much of India's future urban infrastructure and services have yet to be built, creating an opportunity to shape growth in a more resilient way rather than simply retrofitting cities later.

The question is whether India can build not only more cities, but better-functioning urban systems.

That will require engineers and planners, but also public officials, researchers, citizens, civil-society organisations, technology providers, financiers and political leaders.

And it will require them to work together.

The next chapter of sustainable urban development in India may therefore be defined less by the number of projects announced and more by the strength of the systems connecting the people responsible for delivering them.

Because the sustainable city of the future will not be built by infrastructure alone. It will be built by institutions capable of learning, citizens capable of participating, governments capable of coordinating—and networks capable of turning individual effort into collective action.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is sustainable urban development in India?

Sustainable urban development in India means planning and managing cities in ways that balance economic opportunity, social inclusion, environmental sustainability, climate resilience and effective governance. It requires attention not only to infrastructure but also to institutions, public services, citizens and long-term urban systems.

Why are Tier II and Tier III cities important to India's urban future?

India's urbanisation is occurring across a diverse range of settlements, including smaller cities, towns and peri-urban areas. Strengthening these places is therefore important to achieving more balanced and inclusive urban development.

Why is urban governance important for sustainable development?

Many urban challenges cross departmental and administrative boundaries. Effective governance helps coordinate planning, finance, infrastructure, data, service delivery and citizen participation.

How can technology support urban governance?

Digital platforms can improve service delivery, data management, transparency and citizen interaction. India's National Urban Digital Mission is designed around shared digital infrastructure and citizen-centric urban governance.

What is collective action in urban governance?

Collective action involves different stakeholders—such as governments, practitioners, researchers, communities and civil-society organisations—working together around problems that cannot be effectively solved by one institution alone.

Why does citizen participation matter in urban development?

Residents possess practical knowledge about how infrastructure and public services work in everyday life. Meaningful participation can help governments understand local priorities and design more responsive interventions.

What role can urban networks play?

Urban networks can create spaces for peer learning, collaboration, knowledge exchange and coordinated action. U-CAN, for example, describes its role as convening urban practitioners, governments, researchers and philanthropies around shared urban challenges.
