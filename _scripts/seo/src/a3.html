Citizen-Centric Urban Solutions: Designing Indian Cities Around the People Who Live in Them

A citizen-centric city is not simply one that provides more services. It is one that understands how people experience the city—and designs institutions, infrastructure and technology around those everyday realities.

A broken footpath can tell us something about a city's governance.

So can a bus stop where women feel unsafe after dark. A neighbourhood where residents repeatedly complain about flooding. A municipal website that technically offers an online service but is difficult to navigate. A public park that exists on a city map but is inaccessible to older people or persons with disabilities.

These may appear to be individual problems.

They are not.

Together, they reveal whether a city is designed around administrative systems or around the people who use those systems every day.

This is at the heart of citizen-centric urban development.

India's urban transformation is creating enormous opportunities, but it is also placing greater pressure on cities to deliver reliable infrastructure, accessible services, economic opportunity and a better quality of life. The question is therefore moving beyond how much infrastructure cities can build.

It is increasingly about how effectively that infrastructure and those services respond to citizens.

What does “citizen-centric” actually mean?

Citizen-centric urban development is sometimes reduced to consultation: ask residents what they want, collect suggestions and proceed with the project.

Meaningful citizen-centric governance is broader.

It asks:

  • What problem is the resident actually experiencing?
  • How does a person access the service?
  • Who is excluded from the existing system?
  • How much time, money or effort does the citizen have to spend?
  • Can the service be accessed by people with different abilities and levels of digital literacy?
  • Does the government respond when something goes wrong?
  • Can citizens understand how decisions affecting their neighbourhood are made?
  • Can their feedback influence the final decision?

This changes the starting point.

Instead of asking:

“What project should the municipality implement?”

a citizen-centric approach begins with:

“What problem is the resident trying to solve?”

That distinction can fundamentally change urban policy.

From infrastructure-centric to experience-centric cities

For decades, urban development has understandably focused on physical infrastructure.

Cities need roads, drainage, water supply, sewage networks, public transport, housing, parks and waste-management systems.

But infrastructure is only successful when people can use it effectively.

Consider a public transport system.

A city may increase its bus fleet, but a citizen's experience depends on much more:

  • Is the bus stop within reasonable walking distance?
  • Is the route understandable?
  • Is the service reliable?
  • Is the stop accessible?
  • Is the surrounding street safe?
  • Can women use the service comfortably?
  • Can older people board easily?
  • Is information available to people who do not use smartphones?

The infrastructure may exist.

The citizen experience may still be poor.

This is why citizen-centric urban solutions should measure outcomes and experiences, not merely projects completed.

The citizen is not the last link in the system

Traditional public-service delivery can sometimes operate like a chain.

Government designs a programme.

An agency implements it.

A contractor builds the infrastructure.

A department monitors it.

And eventually, the citizen receives the service.

Citizen-centric governance turns that model around.

The citizen becomes part of the design process from the beginning.

That does not mean every municipal decision should be made through public voting. Cities require professional expertise, technical standards, financial discipline and elected decision-making.

It means that citizen experience and local knowledge should become inputs into professional and institutional decision-making.

This is particularly important because administrative data cannot capture everything.

A municipal database may show where roads exist.

Residents can explain where people actually walk.

A drainage map can show infrastructure.

Residents can identify where water accumulates after a particular rainfall event.

A transport plan can show routes.

Commuters can explain where the system breaks down in practice.

The two forms of knowledge are different.

Good urban governance needs both.

Participation must move beyond the public meeting

India has a long history of discussions around decentralisation and citizen participation in urban governance.

NITI Aayog has identified citizen participation as one of the important elements of urban governance and has highlighted challenges associated with fragmented institutions and inadequate opportunities for participatory decision-making. Its work on urban planning has also recommended making planning information easier for citizens to understand and creating greater opportunities for public participation. (NITI Aayog)

The implication is important.

Participation cannot mean simply placing a technical master plan online and asking citizens to comment on it.

Most residents are not trained urban planners.

If citizens are expected to participate, governments must make information understandable.

A neighbourhood plan should be explainable in terms of:

Where will people walk?
Where will children play?
Where will buses stop?
Where will water drain?
Where will trees remain?
Where will shops operate?
What will change for residents?
How much will it cost?
Who will be responsible?

Citizen participation becomes meaningful when people can understand the consequences of a decision.

Designing for the people who are easiest to overlook

A city can appear inclusive when viewed from a distance and remain exclusionary at street level.

Citizen-centric urban solutions therefore have to consider people whose needs are often poorly represented in conventional planning processes.

This includes:

  • women and girls;
  • children;
  • older people;
  • persons with disabilities;
  • informal workers;
  • street vendors;
  • migrants;
  • low-income households;
  • digitally excluded citizens;
  • people living in informal settlements.

The challenge is not simply to create a separate policy for each group.

It is to understand how apparently universal urban systems affect different people differently.

A footpath, for example, is not simply a piece of concrete.

For a wheelchair user, its gradient and continuity matter.

For a parent with a child, crossing design matters.

For an older person, seating and shade may matter.

For a street vendor, regulation and access to customers may matter.

For a woman travelling at night, lighting and surrounding activity may influence whether the route feels usable.

The same infrastructure can therefore produce different experiences for different citizens.

Citizen-centric planning begins by recognising that reality.

Digital government should reduce friction—not create another layer

Technology offers cities an extraordinary opportunity to become more responsive.

India's National Urban Digital Mission (NUDM), launched by the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs, explicitly aims to build shared digital infrastructure for India's urban ecosystem and improve ease of living through accessible, inclusive, efficient and citizen-centric governance. (NUDM)

NUDM also recognises an important principle: citizens should not be forced into a single channel.

Its framework identifies multiple access channels, including citizen service centres, computers, smartphones, local kiosks and doorstep delivery, specifically recognising the needs of digitally excluded sections of society. (NUDM)

This is an important lesson.

Digital-first should not become digital-only.

An online form may be convenient for a digitally confident resident.

For someone without reliable connectivity, a smartphone, digital literacy or familiarity with online government systems, it may become another barrier.

A citizen-centric digital service should therefore ask:

What is the easiest way for different citizens to complete this task?

Sometimes the answer will be an app.

Sometimes it will be a website.

Sometimes it will be a call centre, kiosk, service centre or assisted service.

Good technology disappears into the experience.

The citizen should notice that the service became easier—not that the government adopted a new technology.

Data should help cities listen

Citizen-centric governance also requires better use of data.

Municipalities increasingly have access to information about service requests, property, water supply, waste collection, mobility, infrastructure and public assets.

The opportunity is to use that information to identify patterns.

Suppose thousands of complaints about waterlogging are recorded across several monsoons.

The question should not simply be:

“How quickly did we close the complaints?”

It should also be:

“Why does the same location continue generating complaints?”

This distinction separates complaint management from problem solving.

A citizen-centric city should gradually move from:

complaint → response → closure

towards:

complaint → analysis → root cause → intervention → measurement → learning.

That requires institutional capacity as much as technology.

Feedback is valuable only when something happens afterwards

One of the biggest risks in participatory governance is creating the appearance of participation without creating a mechanism for response.

Citizens provide feedback.

A survey is completed.

A consultation is held.

Suggestions are collected.

And then nothing changes.

This creates frustration rather than trust.

A stronger model establishes a visible feedback loop:

Listen

Understand the problem from citizens and frontline workers.

Analyse

Combine citizen experience with administrative, spatial and technical data.

Decide

Explain what can and cannot be done, and why.

Act

Implement the intervention.

Measure

Assess whether the problem actually improved.

Report back

Tell citizens what changed.

This final step is often overlooked.

A city does not necessarily have to accept every citizen suggestion.

But it should be able to explain its decision.

Accountability is therefore not simply about answering complaints. It is about closing the information loop between government and citizens.

Citizen-centric solutions can also improve government efficiency

Citizen-centricity is sometimes presented as something that makes government slower or more expensive.

The opposite can happen.

If a municipality understands the actual problem before designing a project, it may avoid spending money on an intervention that does not solve the underlying issue.

If digital services are designed around user journeys, fewer citizens may need to visit municipal offices.

If recurring complaints are analysed for root causes, the government can address systemic problems instead of repeatedly responding to symptoms.

If departments share information, citizens may not have to submit the same information multiple times.

This is why citizen-centric governance should not be viewed simply as a “soft” approach.

It can become a mechanism for better public-resource allocation and more effective service delivery.

Water is a good test of citizen-centric urbanism

Water illustrates the difference particularly well.

AMRUT 2.0 focuses on water security and related urban reforms, including water-body rejuvenation, reduction of non-revenue water, reuse of treated water and improvements in municipal services. Its reform framework also includes online municipal services, grievance redressal and GIS-based property mapping. (AMRUT 2.0 Collaboration Platform)

A citizen-centric water programme, however, would ask additional questions.

Is water actually available when households need it?

Is the quality understood and communicated?

Do residents know where to report problems?

Are complaints resolved?

Are low-income neighbourhoods receiving comparable service?

Can citizens understand water conservation measures?

Are residents involved in protecting local water bodies?

This illustrates an important principle:

A city's performance should ultimately be judged at the point where policy meets everyday life.

The smaller-city opportunity

Citizen-centric approaches may be particularly valuable for India's smaller and emerging cities.

The National Urban Digital Mission explicitly identifies the need to make urban digital infrastructure accessible to the “long tail” of smaller cities. (NUDM)

These cities may not have the same financial or technical resources as India's largest metropolitan regions.

That makes collaboration especially important.

A smaller municipality should not necessarily have to develop every digital platform, planning tool, citizen-engagement method or monitoring framework from scratch.

Cities can learn from one another.

States can create shared platforms.

Universities and research organisations can provide technical support.

Civil-society organisations can contribute community knowledge.

Technology organisations can develop reusable tools.

Practitioners can document what works—and, equally importantly, what does not.

This is where urban networks and learning communities can become useful infrastructure.

The role of collaboration

Urban problems rarely fit neatly inside one organisation.

A city may need the municipal corporation, development authority, transport agency, water utility, police, elected representatives, community organisations, researchers and private-sector actors to coordinate around a single problem.

Yet institutional fragmentation can make this difficult.

NITI Aayog has identified overlapping jurisdictions and fragmented roles among urban agencies as challenges for effective city governance. Its 2026 framework on million-plus city governments similarly identifies institutional fragmentation, uneven devolution, limited capacity and unclear responsibilities as structural governance challenges. (NITI Aayog)

This is why citizen-centric urban solutions should not be understood simply as better-designed services.

They also require better-designed relationships between institutions.

If a resident reports a problem that falls between two departments, the citizen should not have to understand the government's organisational chart.

The government should.

From “citizen participation” to “citizen partnership”

There is a useful distinction between participation and partnership.

Participation can mean asking citizens for opinions.

Partnership means creating mechanisms through which citizens, communities and institutions contribute to solving a problem.

The difference is substantial.

Imagine a neighbourhood struggling with waste management.

A participation model might ask residents whether they are satisfied with garbage collection.

A partnership model might bring residents, sanitation workers, municipal officials, waste-management organisations and local institutions together to identify the causes, redesign collection practices, improve segregation and monitor results.

The second model recognises that citizens are not merely consumers of municipal services.

They can also be co-producers of better urban outcomes.

That does not transfer the government's responsibilities to citizens.

Rather, it recognises that some urban problems can be solved more effectively when government capacity and community knowledge work together.

What should a citizen-centric urban solution look like?

There is no universal formula.

But a practical framework can begin with eight questions:

1. Start with the problem

Define the citizen's actual problem rather than beginning with a predetermined project.

2. Understand different users

Map how women, men, children, older people, persons with disabilities, workers and low-income residents may experience the same service differently.

3. Combine data with lived experience

Use administrative and technical data alongside local knowledge.

4. Design for multiple access channels

Do not assume that every citizen has the same device, connectivity or digital literacy.

5. Make government information understandable

Technical plans should be translated into language and visual formats citizens can use.

6. Create a feedback loop

Citizens should know what happened after they provided feedback.

7. Measure outcomes

Ask whether the resident's problem actually improved.

8. Share the learning

Document what worked, what failed and what other cities can adapt.

A citizen-centricity checklist for urban organisations

Before launching an urban initiative, governments, NGOs, researchers, philanthropies and urban practitioners can ask:

Problem

  • What specific citizen problem are we solving?

Users

  • Who experiences the problem?
  • Who may be excluded from our proposed solution?

Access

  • Can citizens access the service through more than one channel?

Participation

  • At what stage can citizens influence the process?

Evidence

  • What data supports the proposed intervention?

Accountability

  • Who is responsible for implementation?

Feedback

  • How will citizens know what happened after they provide feedback?

Outcome

  • What measurable change should citizens experience?

Learning

  • Can the approach be adapted by another neighbourhood or city?

If these questions cannot be answered, the initiative may still be a useful project—but it may not yet be a genuinely citizen-centric solution.

The future of Indian cities will be measured at street level

Urban policy is often discussed in terms of large numbers: kilometres of roads, houses sanctioned, projects completed, investments mobilised, kilometres of pipeline laid or digital platforms launched.

These measurements matter.

But citizens experience cities differently.

They experience them while walking to work.

Waiting for a bus.

Collecting water.

Taking a child to school.

Visiting a public hospital.

Running a small business.

Crossing a road.

Reporting a broken streetlight.

Finding a public toilet.

Looking for a safe public space.

Trying to understand a government process.

This is where the success of urban policy ultimately becomes visible.

A citizen-centric city therefore does not necessarily mean a city where every citizen gets everything they request.

It means a city where institutions listen systematically, explain decisions, design services around real needs, include people who are often overlooked, respond to problems and learn from experience.

That requires more than technology.

It requires more than infrastructure.

And it requires more than consultation.

It requires a shift in the way cities think about governance itself.

The future of citizen-centric urban development in India will depend on moving from designing cities for citizens to designing cities with citizens—and building the institutional capacity to turn that participation into better urban outcomes.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are citizen-centric urban solutions?

Citizen-centric urban solutions are policies, services, infrastructure and governance approaches designed around the actual needs and experiences of people living in cities. They combine service quality, accessibility, participation, accountability, technology and evidence.

Why is citizen participation important in urban governance?

Citizens experience public services directly and possess local knowledge that may not be visible in administrative datasets. Meaningful participation can help governments understand local problems and improve planning and service delivery. NITI Aayog has identified citizen participation as an important component of effective urban governance. (NITI Aayog)

Does citizen-centric governance mean citizens make every decision?

No. Citizen-centric governance does not eliminate professional, administrative or elected decision-making. It means that citizen needs, experiences and feedback are systematically considered within decision-making processes.

Can technology make cities more citizen-centric?

Yes, when technology reduces barriers and improves access, transparency and service delivery. India's National Urban Digital Mission specifically aims to support accessible, inclusive, efficient and citizen-centric urban governance. (NUDM)

What does “digital-first” mean for citizens without internet access?

A citizen-centric approach should not assume universal digital access. NUDM's principles recognise multiple access channels, including service centres, computers, smartphones, kiosks and doorstep delivery, with the objective of reaching digitally excluded citizens. (NUDM)

How can cities measure whether they are citizen-centric?

Cities can measure service reliability, accessibility, response times, grievance resolution, user satisfaction, inclusion, participation and—most importantly—whether the underlying problem experienced by residents has improved.

Why are citizen-centric solutions important for smaller cities?

Smaller and emerging cities often face different combinations of institutional, financial and technical constraints. Shared digital infrastructure, peer learning, collaboration and reusable solutions can help them strengthen service delivery without requiring every city to build every capability independently.

What is the difference between citizen participation and citizen partnership?

Participation generally involves citizens contributing views or feedback. Partnership goes further by creating mechanisms for citizens, communities, government and other stakeholders to work together on identifying, implementing and monitoring solutions.

Conclusion

India's urban future will not be determined only by how many projects cities build.

It will also be determined by whether those projects solve the problems people actually experience.

A truly citizen-centric city is therefore not defined by the number of apps it launches, consultations it conducts or dashboards it publishes.

It is defined by something much more fundamental:

Can a person living in that city experience government as accessible, understandable, responsive and accountable?

That is the real test of citizen-centric urban development.

And it may be one of the most important tests of India's urban transformation.

Author's note

This article uses the term “citizen-centric” in the context of urban governance and service delivery. The approach draws on Government of India frameworks, including the National Urban Digital Mission and AMRUT 2.0, as well as NITI Aayog's work on urban governance and citizen participation. The article's recommendations are analytical synthesis rather than claims that any single government programme has already achieved all of these outcomes.
